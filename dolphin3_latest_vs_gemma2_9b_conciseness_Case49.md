In a regional banking network experiencing an NPL spike exceeding 7.5%, the structural liquidity drop can be quantified as follows:

1. The total loan portfolio is assumed to be $1 trillion for simplicity.
2. The NPL ratio is 7.5%, so the NPL amount is $75 billion.
3. The loss in value due to NPLs is 15% of the loan portfolio, which is $150 billion.
4. The bank's capital is $100 billion, which is insufficient to cover the $150 billion loss.
5. The bank's liquidity drops by $150 billion due to the NPLs.

When $10.5 billion is locked into illiquid offshore assets, the liquidity further drops by $10.5 billion, resulting in a total liquidity drop of $160.5 billion.

In a domestic banking system with an 83.4% dollarization rate, a 10% cash withdrawal run by local depositors amounts to $200 billion. Since institutional currency reserves are gated, the bank cannot meet this withdrawal, leading to a complete freeze of assets.

The resulting capital insolvency can be calculated as follows:

1. The bank's capital is $100 billion.
2. The total liabilities are $1 trillion.
3. The bank's equity is zero, indicating complete capital insolvency.

The physical mechanism of the asset freeze is as follows:

1. The bank's assets are frozen due to the inability to meet the withdrawal demands.
2. The bank's offshore assets are illiquid and cannot be converted into cash.
3. The bank's real estate assets are in bankruptcy or restructuring, further reducing its liquidity.
4. The bank's remaining assets are insufficient to cover the withdrawal demands, leading to a complete freeze.

In summary, the bank experiences a $160.5 billion liquidity drop, a 15% loss in value due to NPLs, and a complete capital insolvency of $1 trillion. The physical mechanism of the asset freeze is due to the inability to meet withdrawal demands and the illiquidity of the bank's assets.
